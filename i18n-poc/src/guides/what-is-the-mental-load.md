You're in a meeting when it surfaces, unbidden: the passport expires before the summer trip. Later, in the shower: the class-trip deposit is due Friday. Over dinner, mid-sentence: there's nothing defrosted for tomorrow. None of these are tasks you're *doing* — they're tasks you're *holding*, a background process that never quite closes. That background process has a name. It's called the mental load, and once you can see it, you can't unsee it.

Most conversations about who does what at home count the visible work: who cooked, who drove, who folded the laundry. The mental load is the layer underneath — the noticing, planning, remembering and deciding that has to happen *before* any of that visible work gets done. It's real, it's measurable, and research is increasingly clear about who carries most of it.

## What "the mental load" actually means

The sharpest definition comes from sociologist Allison Daminger, whose 2019 study in the *American Sociological Review* gave the concept its academic backbone. She breaks what she calls **cognitive labour** into four steps that repeat, invisibly, all day long:

**Anticipating** a need before it becomes urgent — noticing the school shoes are getting tight before they don't fit. **Identifying** the options — which shop, which size, whether there's time before Saturday. **Deciding** among them — actually making the call. And **monitoring** the outcome — checking it got done, and starting the loop again.

Only that third step, the decision, tends to be shared or even noticed. The anticipating, identifying and monitoring happen silently in one person's head. That's why the mental load feels so heavy and looks like nothing: it's the three-quarters of the work that never shows up as an event on anyone's calendar.

## Why it still falls mostly on mothers

Here's where the research gets uncomfortable. A 2024 study by Ana Catalano Weeks and colleagues in the *Journal of Marriage and Family*, surveying 3,000 parents, found that **mothers carry 71% of the household mental load** — roughly 60% more than fathers. Pew Research Center data tells the same story from a different angle: among couples who both work full-time, mothers are consistently more likely to be the one managing the day-to-day of family life.

And it isn't simply a function of who earns less or works fewer hours. Follow-up research by the same team — pointedly titled *Take a Load Off? Not for Mothers* — found that neither more money nor more time reliably closes the gap. The mental load clings to the person who has become the family's default organiser, largely regardless of income or career.

That default is *usually* a mother — but not always, and this is worth saying plainly. In plenty of homes the load sits with whoever runs the logistics: the parent who travels less, the single earner, the one who quietly became the household's operating system. If your family lives across two homes or two countries, it's often whoever holds the whole picture in their head. The gendered pattern is real and well-documented; so is the fact that the weight, wherever it lands, shouldn't sit on one set of shoulders.

## What the mental load looks like in an ordinary week

Strip away the abstraction and it's disarmingly concrete. It's knowing when the school holiday starts and when the passport expires. It's remembering that one child needs a costume by Thursday and the other has swimming, that the parcel needs signing for, that your mother's birthday is next week and you haven't sorted anything. It's the running tally of what's in the fridge and what that means for dinner. Each item is small. The weight isn't in any one of them — it's in being the only person tracking all of them at once, with no off switch.

## The toll it takes

Carrying this invisibly, indefinitely, has a measurable cost. The *State of Motherhood in Europe 2024* report from Make Mothers Matter — a survey of nearly 10,000 mothers across eleven EU countries and the UK — found that **67% of mothers feel chronically overloaded**, and **half reported mental health problems** such as anxiety, depression or burnout linked to it. The strain fell hardest on single mothers and those in lower-income households.

The knock-on effects reach beyond wellbeing. Gallup has found that working mothers are roughly twice as likely as fathers to consider cutting their hours or leaving their jobs because of family responsibilities. The mental load, in other words, isn't only exhausting — it quietly shapes careers.

## Why it's so hard to just share

If the load is this heavy, why doesn't it get handed round more easily? Because you can't delegate what the other person can't see. "Just tell me what to do" sounds like help, but it keeps the anticipating, identifying and monitoring — the actual load — exactly where it was. The person asking still has to hold the whole system in their head and then *manage the manager*. Sharing a single task isn't the same as sharing responsibility for a domain.

Real relief comes from making the invisible visible, and from transferring whole areas of responsibility rather than one-off chores. Not "can you pick up milk," but "you own everything to do with the kids' medical appointments — the noticing, the booking, all of it." That's the shift that actually lightens the head, rather than just the hands.

## How to actually start sharing it

A few things genuinely help. **Get it out of your head and into a shared place** — a system everyone can see beats a mental list only one person holds. **Hand over domains, not tasks**, so the anticipating and remembering move too. **Make the invisible steps explicit**, naming the noticing and planning as real work rather than pretending the job is only the visible bit. And **let a system do the remembering** where it can, so being organised doesn't depend on one person never dropping the thread.

That last point is where technology can help — if it's built for it. Most family apps are just shared calendars: a better place to store the load, but you're still the one who has to notice, type in and track everything. The harder, more useful problem is taking some of the noticing off your plate entirely.
